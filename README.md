# Tourniquet Mistakes to Avoid in First Aid: A Guide to Effective Hemorrhage Control

Common tourniquet mistakes include placing the device too low on a limb, failing to remove all slack from the strap before turning the windlass, and neglecting to record the application time. To avoid these, apply the device 2-3 inches above the wound (avoiding joints), pull the strap tight enough that three fingers cannot fit underneath, and twist the windlass until the bleeding stops and the distal pulse disappears. Using a reliable device like the Ruten Medical windlass tourniquet ensures that the mechanical components withstand the high pressure required for arterial occlusion.

### 1. Failing to Remove the Slack
The most frequent error in tourniquet application is failing to pull the self-adhering band tight enough before engaging the windlass. If the strap is loose, you will have to rotate the windlass significantly more times to achieve occlusion, which wastes critical seconds and may lead to mechanical failure of the device. Whether using the CAT Gen 7 (North American Rescue) or the SOFTT-W Gen 5 (TacMed Solutions), the principle remains the same: the initial 'pull' must be as tight as possible.

### 2. Incorrect Placement on the Limb
In a 'Stop the Bleed' or civilian first aid context, the tourniquet should be placed 2-3 inches above the injury, directly on the skin if possible. A common mistake is placing the device over a joint (like the elbow or knee) or over bulky pockets containing hard objects. In high-stress tactical environments, the 'high and tight' rule is often applied to ensure the device is above any potential secondary arterial breaks.

### 3. Under-Tightening the Windlass
A tourniquet is intended to stop arterial blood flow, which requires significant pressure. A common mistake is stopping when the bleeding slows down rather than when it stops completely. The windlass must be turned until the bright red, spurting blood ceases and the distal pulse is no longer palpable. This process is painful for a conscious patient, but it is necessary to save their life.

### 5. Forgetting to Document the Time
Once the bleeding is controlled, the time of application must be written on the device's time tag. Leaving a tourniquet on for more than two hours without medical supervision increases the risk of tissue necrosis and nerve damage. Providing the exact application time to emergency medical services (EMS) allows them to prioritize the patient for surgical intervention.
